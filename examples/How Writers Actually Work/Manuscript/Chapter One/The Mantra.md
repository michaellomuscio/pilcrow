Almost every piece of writing advice you have ever been given traces back to the same handful of studies, and almost nobody who repeats the advice has read them.

The instruction is *write every day*. It is repeated in books, in workshops, and in the interface of nearly every writing application on the market, usually in the form of a streak counter that turns red when you miss.

The evidence underneath it is thinner than the confidence suggests. Only about one academic in eight writes daily, and daily writing turns out to be neither a reliable marker nor a predictor of productivity [@sword2016, p. 316]. The studies the mantra rests on were small, self-selected, and never replicated [@boice1989].

What does hold up is more specific and less punishing. Naming a time and a place in advance — an if-then plan rather than a target — raises follow-through substantially across hundreds of studies [@gollwitzer2006]. And the strongest single predictor of a good day in creative work is not output at all, but the sense of having made progress on something that matters [@amabile2011].
